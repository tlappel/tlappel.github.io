I used to hide my badge at industry conferences.

Not because I wasn't supposed to be there — I'd registered like everyone else. I hid it because the badge said which organization I worked for, and in the building automation world, that marked me as an "End User." I heard that term tossed around at those events like a polite way of saying *idiot*. The whole industry was built on an assumption: the owner is not the expert. The owner buys what the contractor recommends, waits for the contractor to fix things, and doesn't ask too many questions. The owner needed to not have access because they would create more problems, they would edit code or make changes that would result in calls. Whatever the case was, they shouldn't have admin rights to their own stuff. 

I felt genuinely enraged by that. And it became my mission to run circles around the only people I could get resources from.

## How I got to that conference

My path didn't start in buildings. I was a helicopter mechanic in the Marine Corps, then built wind turbines on the assembly line, and eventually worked my way into the manufacturer's Remote Monitoring and Diagnostics Center — watching machine data all day, diagnosing faults on turbines hundreds of miles away.

That's where I hit my ceiling. I was talking to engineering daily, and I could feel the edge of what I knew. I wanted what they had. I initially figured that meant electrical or mechanical engineering — then I took my first computer science course and realized I'd found the thing. I finished a Bachelor of Science in Computer Science while working, while being a father to small children, and in my senior year at Mount Mercy University, I built an intermediate-level compiler in Python for a custom language my professor designed. I learned more doing that than in any other single project, and it was fun the whole way through.

Then I got laid off — and landed at a community college that had, poetically, purchased a turbine from the very manufacturer that let me go.

## Beyond the thermostat

The college introduced me to building automation, and I quickly realized there was an entire world I had never seen beyond the thermostat: programming, engineering, data analysis, tuning. Coming from turbine diagnostics, it felt familiar — machines producing data, data telling stories, most people not listening.

I became hyper-focused on Niagara, the framework underneath it all. At least where I worked. Getting resources as an owner meant jumping through hoops the industry never designed for me. So I jumped through them anyway. I'm now a certified Niagara developer — a credential most contracting companies don't have on staff themselves — building Java enterprise-style applications on the platform, extending the framework. Along the way, the relationships changed: I was told more than once that I was "not the typical customer," and eventually the walls came down far enough that we could purchase direct from OEMs and get proper training and tooling ourselves.

The industry called me an End User. Fine. I became an end user who could out-build the experts.

## Restarting the journey

All of that — the diagnostics years, the roll-your-own tools (Excel macros, LabVIEW, SQL, then Java and Python), the stubbornness about being underestimated — points at the same next step.

I'm restarting my Machine Learning and AI journey, deliberately this time. I'm working through the [fast.ai course](https://course.fast.ai/Lessons/lesson1.html) (the [book is free on GitHub](https://github.com/fastai/fastbook)), and I'm building AI and reasoning projects in the building automation space — systems that don't just forward an alarm, but understand the context around it.

This blog documents that: the lessons, the projects, the dead ends, the wins.

Same mission as the conferences, really. Just a bigger room.
